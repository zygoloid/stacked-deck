# Stacked Deck: the story

You've been hired to run the floor at **The Stacked Deck**, an esoteric
casino where every croupier knows exactly one move. You never meet the
**Owner**. Instructions arrive as notes under the cash tray, each sealed with
an ace of spades. Over four suit chapters the casino goes from a quiet opening
night to running the city; then an envelope with your name on it opens the
finale. Every floor is a playing card (see docs/campaign.md): hearts, then
diamonds, clubs and spades, and four special cards at the end.

Tone: light and dry. Each floor's story is one to three short paragraphs that
set the scene, add a beat to the plot, and introduce any new croupier. It
never explains how to solve the floor.

## Cast

* **The Owner**: unseen. Signs every note with the ace of spades, which is
  also the house's calling card. Fond of the number seven. The last envelope
  is addressed to you.
* **Old Thursday**: a retired stage magician who has bet on hearts for forty
  years. Pulls sevens out of mayors' ears. Lives on a pension.
* **The Marchetti twins**: two tables, one desk, no patience.
* **Dmitri**: an accountant who plays exactly one hour a night, likes even
  numbers and spreadsheets.
* **The Vellanis**: the diamond family from across the river: Sal (three gold
  watches) and his uncle (strict about greed, waiting forty years for a royal
  flush). They like red.
* **The Kessler crew**: from the coal yards. They like black, deal from the
  bottom, and are sure the house hides aces.
* **The doormen**: invented Top Dog on their break. The head doorman calls
  himself the top dog.
* **A minor royal** from somewhere small and mountainous, with very still
  bodyguards.
* **Commissioner Pryce**: from the city Gaming Commission. He takes envelopes,
  writes regulations, and likes to win on Tuesdays.
* **The rival casino** two blocks away (all neon), and its owner, who ends up
  dealing for you.
* **The mayor**: likes records. Keeps a tally on a napkin.

## Chapter 1 ♥ Opening Night (pass, suits, straight, shoe/gather/cut)

The casino opens. Cards only need to reach the cashier: around a bar the Owner
had put in overnight, then from two tables at once. Old Thursday wants his
hearts paid, and you learn that a port with no direction drops cards on the
carpet. Sal Vellani wants red, the Kesslers black: the families are circling.
Then the Owner's notes turn to the number seven, and you learn to stack a
reference card from a shoe on a player's card, test the pair and cut the
reference off again. Old Thursday plays penny ante, a minor royal wants court
cards, and Dmitri's even-money bet closes the chapter while the Vellanis start
asking who he is.

## Chapter 2 ♦ Stacking the Deck (flush, n of a kind, War)

The Vellanis arrive in force. Sal's uncle has games, opinions and forty years
of patience; Dmitri has a spreadsheet about sevens; the doormen have a game of
their own. A new croupier plays War, and the house learns to compare a hand
with a constant, with its own pieces, and finally to rig a War so it must be
lost. The chapter ends with the Vellani family game on the whole floor, and
two blocks away a rival casino switches on its neon.

## Chapter 3 ♣ House Rules (duplicate, set, top-card War, shuffles, deal)

The rival casino opens with a brass band and free shrimp, and the regulars
waver; the Owner answers with ever more generous house games, and you learn
to copy a hand and let the copy decide. Commissioner Pryce takes an envelope
and leaves a regulation: War by the top card only (4♣ to 9♣). The Kesslers'
bottom dealer and his cousin get their own games, so you learn the riffles,
and the string quartet's second violin gets his. The knockout brings the best
card to the top; Pryce's Tuesday game, Low Ball, turns it upside down, and
Pryce loses it. The next day the regulation is withdrawn "pending review".
The chapter ends with verdict cards: a copy of the hand decides, a released
card carries the verdict, and the original is paid or not at a gate. Old
Thursday comes back from the rival's, and the rival throws its last big night.

## Chapter 4 ♠ The House Always Wins (memory, burn, the door, sort, hand)

The rival casino's neon goes dark and its owner now deals for you on
Tuesdays, bringing his habit of burning a card and, later, a video poker
machine. "The house remembers": the mayor's records and superstitions need
memory, a shoe that deals once, and shoe decks that run in order. The council
meets in the back room over a full house, admitted through the ticket door.
Old Thursday wants the wheel; the Vellanis and the Kesslers stop fighting and
both wear spades in their hatbands. Dmitri's streak theory and the doormen's
game across hands carry verdicts from one hand to the next, and the mayor's
napkin grows a column for neighbours. The final game is a record table with
the whole city at it; afterwards an envelope with your name on it waits
under the cash tray, sealed with an ace of spades.

## Chapter 5 The Envelope (special cards)

The envelope holds the deeds, the key to the cash tray and a note in
handwriting very like your own: you are the Owner now. The printers send the
new house deck, and it has extras nobody ordered: two jokers (one plain, one
in every colour) and two advertising cards. Only shoes deal them. Jokers are
wild reference cards; advertising cards are worth nothing, so they mark and
seed without ever making a pair. Each finale floor introduces one of them.
The last card in the box carries the last note, sealed with the ace of spades
and signed by you, describing a game the whole city has been playing since
opening night.

## Notes for writers

* Keep each floor's story to one to three short paragraphs. New croupiers and
  cards are introduced in a sentence, in the story of the floor that unlocks
  them. Special cards are a reveal: no floor before `?` mentions jokers or
  advertising cards (test/levels.js checks the words).
* The brief is the precise rule. The story can be playful, but it must never
  contradict the brief or explain the solution. Edge cases (aces high or low,
  ties go to the house, two pair versus trips, the ace-to-five straight)
  belong in the story or the brief whenever a player could reasonably guess
  wrong.
